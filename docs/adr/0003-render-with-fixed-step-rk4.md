# Render with fixed-step RK4, and keep the adaptive solver for the search

The project has two integrators. The launch solution search uses `solve_ivp`,
which runs RK45 with a tolerance controller and a step cap. The real-time
renderer steps semi-implicit Euler once per frame. Both call
`BallisticPhysics.compute_acceleration`, so the physics under them is identical
and the step rule is the only thing separating their answers.

We measured the gap instead of arguing about it. For one launch (300 m/s at 35
degrees, the default atmosphere and wind table) we swept the step size across
three orders of magnitude for each rule and compared every result against an RK45
reference integrated to `rtol = atol = 1e-12`, landing at a 3611 m range after
26.384 s. The figure is `figures/integrator_convergence.png`, regenerated by
`scripts/integrator_convergence.py`.

What came back:

- Semi-implicit Euler has observed order 1.00 across the whole sweep. At the
  60 fps frame step it lands 5.14 m from where the adaptive solver lands. Sixteen
  substeps per frame brings that to 0.32 m.
- Fixed-step RK4 lands 7.3e-6 m at the 60 fps frame step, five orders of
  magnitude closer than Euler, and its error falls monotonically enough over the
  coarse end of the sweep to fit order 3.34 rather than the method's nominal 4.
  The gap between 4 and 3.34 is the wind table: the acceleration is only
  piecewise smooth in altitude, because the table changes slope at each of its
  five levels. Below about 0.003 s the RK4 error is inside the reference's own
  accuracy and the curve stops being a measurement of the rule.
- Capping RK45's step anywhere from 0.1 s to 0.0001 s changes nothing. The error
  scatters between 2e-9 and 3e-7 m with no trend in the step size, because the
  cap is always looser than the step the tolerance controller picks on its own.

So the 5.14 m was never a mystery and it was never physics. It was the price of
a first-order rule evaluated once per frame.

## The decision

The renderer steps with fixed-step RK4, through
`BallisticPhysics.integrate_fixed_step`. It still takes a known number of steps
of a known size, decided before the frame starts, so frame pacing stays exactly
as deterministic as the single Euler step it replaces. The gap between what the
solver recommends and what the sandbox draws drops from 5.14 m to under a
hundredth of a millimetre.

It costs 27 microseconds per projectile per frame instead of 6, measured on this
machine. A 60 fps frame has 16.7 ms, so the frame budget is nowhere near
interesting yet, but the cost is real and it grows linearly with the number of
projectiles in flight.

The tolerance-calibration alternative was rejected on the numbers. Reaching
0.32 m with Euler costs sixteen substeps per frame and sixteen acceleration
evaluations, and it buys a worse answer than one RK4 step does for four. Because
Euler is first order, every further order of accuracy costs a factor of ten in
substeps. Calibrating a first-order rule is buying accuracy at a bad exchange
rate, and the study says the rate is bad.

**Considered options:** calibrate the realtime step with a tolerance test and
leave the rule alone, and unify on one fixed-step integrator throughout. The
first is above. The second is half done here and is described under consequences.

## Why not adaptive in the renderer

This was settled before the study and the study did not reopen it. An adaptive
controller chooses its steps from an error estimate, so the number of steps a
frame needs is not known until the frame is over. That makes the renderer's cost
per frame variable, which is the one thing a real-time loop cannot afford. A
projectile near its apex takes different steps than one in the descent, so the
frame rate would breathe as the shot develops. The renderer exists to show a
flight in real time, and a real-time loop that runs at a different speed
depending on where the projectile is has stopped being one.

Fixed-step RK4 keeps the property. The step count is chosen before the frame
starts and never varies.

The measurement also turned out to undercut the reason for keeping two
integrators at all. Every trajectory in the project is integrated with
`max_step` between 0.05 and 0.1 s, and that cap binds: at 0.05 s the solver takes
533 steps for a 26 s flight, so it is already a fixed-step integrator with a
tolerance safety net rather than an adaptive one. Spending the same 0.05 s
budget on RK4 lands within 7.3e-5 m, against 1.1e-6 m for the capped RK45. Both
are orders of magnitude below the 20 m target radius, so the search would lose
nothing that anything in the project can observe, and unification would cost it
no extra evaluations.

**Consequences:** the renderer half of the unification is done, because it was a
loop of five lines in one place. The search half is not, and the numbers say it
is affordable rather than necessary: replacing `trajectory_3d` means replacing
the solver's entire integration path, including the ground-impact event
handling, behind every search and every physics test, which is a different piece
of work from measuring the divergence and deciding what to do about it. Until it
happens, two integration paths remain in the codebase, so a future change to the
acceleration model can still move the renderer's answer without moving the
solver's by the same amount.

The renderer's stepping now lives in the physics module rather than in the
renderer, so the study, the test, and the frame loop all exercise the same
function. `tests/test_integrator_divergence.py` pins the frame-step agreement, so
a change that breaks it fails a test rather than showing up as a drifting
screenshot. Separately, RK4 does not reach its nominal order in this problem,
and the reason is the wind table rather than the rule: a spline through the
levels instead of a piecewise-linear interpolation would remove the kinks, at the
cost of changing the physics rather than the numerics.
